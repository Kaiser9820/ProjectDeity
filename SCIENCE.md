# Scientific data and model

## Bodies
The Sun and the eight planets (`src/data/solarSystem.ts`). Physical values (mass, GM, radii, density,
sidereal rotation, obliquity, albedo) are copied from the JPL Horizons physical-data pages for targets
10, 199, 299, 399, 499, 599, 699, 799 and 899 (retrieved 2026-10-28). The reference orbital elements
shown in the inspector come from JPL *Approximate Positions of the Planets*, Table 1 (J2000). They are
for display only. The integrator never reads them.

## Initial conditions
`src/data/horizons-2026-01-01.json` is generated by `node scripts/fetch-horizons.mjs` from the JPL
Horizons API:

| | |
|---|---|
| Epoch | 2026-01-01 00:00 TDB (JD 2461041.5) |
| Frame | Ecliptic and mean equinox of J2000.0 (ICRF) |
| Origin | Solar System Barycentre (500@0) |
| Units | au, au/day |
| Correction | none (geometric states) |

Planets with moons enter the model as **system barycentres** (Horizons 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8), paired with
system GM values from DE440 (JPL Astrodynamic Parameters). That way each point mass and its gravity are
consistent. Earth is the Earth–Moon barycentre with GM(Earth)+GM(Moon). Earth's centre lies ≈4 670 km
from that point, which is less than one Earth radius.

## Dynamics
`src/physics/NBodySystem.ts` uses Newtonian point-mass gravity between all pairs, in units of au, day
and GM [au³/day²]. GM values are converted from km³/s² using 1 au = 149 597 870.7 km. The integrator is
velocity Verlet (symplectic, 2nd order) with a fixed 1/16-day step. A fixed-timestep accumulator
(`SimulationClock`) separates simulated time from the frame rate. The model uses no softening and no
correction forces. If two bodies' physical radii touch, or any value becomes non-finite, the simulation
halts and keeps its last valid state.

## Interactive edits
Moving a body with the gizmo or typed coordinates calls `GravitySimulation.setBodyPosition`. That
writes the physical position in the N-body state, keeps the body's velocity, GM and radius, and
recomputes accelerations. Integration is held during a drag (the user's pause/run choice is
unchanged). A move that would overlap another body is rejected with a message, and the state is left
unchanged. After the edit, the body follows gravity from its new state. Nothing corrects it back. Orbit
paths are re-predicted after every edit. Reset restores the Horizons epoch.

## Validation (`tests/solarSystem.test.ts`)
The test integrates 365 days and compares the result with Horizons states at 2027-01-01 TDB
(`tests/fixtures/horizons-2027-01-01.json`). Measured position errors:

| Body | Error after 1 yr |
|---|---|
| Mercury | ≈ 9 600 km |
| Venus | ≈ 1 100 km |
| Earth–Moon barycentre | ≈ 420 km |
| Mars | ≈ 100 km |
| Jupiter … Neptune | ≤ 1 km |

The relative energy error stays below 1e-8 over 50 simulated years.

## Known limitations
- The model leaves out general relativity. This is the main source of Mercury's error, roughly 43″ per
  century of perihelion precession.
- It also leaves out the Moon as a separate body, asteroids, Pluto and other small bodies, planetary
  oblateness, and solar mass loss.
- Errors grow with simulated time. This is a teaching and sandbox model, **not** a substitute for JPL
  DE ephemerides, and it does not provide exact long-term predictions.
- The displayed date is TDB. TDB differs from UTC by about 69 s.
- Orbit paths come from integrating a copy of the current state forward for one sidereal period of each
  planet, using a 1-day step. They are refreshed on reset and every 20 simulated years.

## Presentation (`src/scene/presentation.ts`)
- 1 scene unit = 10⁶ km, linear, with no spatial compression.
- Body size is a visual preset (toolbar): True size, ×100, ×500 or ×1000 (the default). All planets
  share one factor, so they stay proportional to each other. The Sun has its own smaller factor (10×,
  20×, 45×, 55×) so it never reaches Mercury's perihelion. At the larger presets, Jupiter and Saturn
  therefore look as large as the Sun or larger. Orbits and distances are never scaled. Presets never
  affect gravity, contact detection or displayed values.
- Axial tilt is applied relative to the ecliptic, and rotation phase is arbitrary. Both are visual only.
- Surface maps come from Solar System Scope (CC BY 4.0) and are compiled from NASA imagery. The Uranus
  and Neptune maps are nearly featureless because the real planets show little resolved detail.
